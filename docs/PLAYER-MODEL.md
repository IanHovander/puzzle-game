# PLAYER-MODEL — *What the Fire Keeps*

> ## ⚠ TOTAL SPOILERS
> Every reveal in the game, including the Prologue's, the Finale's and all five endings. Written for
> the author and collaborators. Do not show it to a player or a host. Companion to `CANON.md` (what is
> true) and the nine `epistemics-*.md` files (who believes what, and what they think others believe).

**This document is about the reader's head, not the characters'.** Nothing below is a claim about the
world. Where a row says the player believes a thing, the truth is in `CANON.md` and is restated only
where the error needs it. Where a row says a character knows a thing, the receipt is in the relevant
`epistemics-*.md`. What is modelled here is **four real people at a table with one laptop and four
phones**, over about four hours: what they know, what they are guessing, which guesses we want them to
keep, which we want killed and by exactly what evidence, and which breadcrumbs make the answers feel
earned rather than sprung.

Two standing facts govern every table below.

1. **The partition is absolute.** "Say what you see. Never show your phone." (`lore.js:74`), reprinted
   above every SPEAK page. Every hypothesis is really *four* hypotheses until somebody talks. "What the
   player knows" therefore means *what four people can reconstruct by asking each other*, which is
   strictly less than the union of four phones — and the gap between those two quantities is where most
   of this document's findings live.
2. **Nobody is taking notes.** The Book tab is promised as the memory (`ch0.js:183`) and the game says
   twice that nothing needs writing down (`ch0.js:65`, `:183`). Tables take notes anyway, from `ch2`
   onward. Plan for it.

**Three tags are used throughout, as the brief requires.** **[WITHHOLDING]** = mystery with no basis to
speculate. **[UNEARNED]** = a reveal that lands without breadcrumbs the player could have found.
**[PROPOSED]** = my line, not the shipped game's; the author accepts or rejects.

**Cross-references.** `A-I §n` = `player-act1.md`; `A-II §n` = `player-act2.md`; `A-III §n` =
`player-act3.md`; `§12.n` / `§13.n` unqualified = `CANON.md`.

# 1. THE INTENDED EXPERIENCE IN ELEVEN LINES

*If this page is not compelling, the game is not compelling. Written as prose, one paragraph per spine
point, describing the feeling the design is buying and the suspicion it is planting.*

**T0 — before play.** Four people are doing admin. They are scanning a QR code, arguing about seats, and
reading a fair-warning notice out loud in a joke-ominous voice. Nothing is at stake and nobody is
invested. The only two things that should stick are the tagline — *"A night in four hands"* — and the
one proper noun in the warning: *the bells of Thornhallow will ring.* Both are true statements of the
ending, delivered as packaging. The intended feeling is mild, pleasant conspiracy: **we are four, and
the game has told us in a safety notice that one day it will need all four of us at once.**

**T1 — the cold open and the stone.** Six slow centred lines over a great orange fire, and the room goes
quiet. Four people closed a wound; they left a fire on top; it went out once, fourteen years ago, and
when it came back there was a baby on the stones; that child is Wren, and *so are you* — fourteen. The
player should feel the specific pleasure of being handed something enormous in flat, unhurried prose.
Then the prophecy, in the school's translation, with an art caption that is a byline: **THE ORDER'S
READING**. The intended suspicion is a triangle, and the player should hold all three corners at once:
*the fire made this child*, *the translation is contested*, and *everybody in this building has already
decided this child is the sacrifice.* One hypothesis must die early and does — the mundane foundling —
and the player should not yet be able to say why they no longer believe it.

**T2 — the lamp lights, and the four speak.** A four-hundred-year-old lamp nobody has ever lit opens to
four hands and four gifts, and says two words: *Fire, keep.* The intended feeling here is **competence
and belonging**: the table has just done something the entire school cannot, by talking to each other.
Then the game takes it away. Each of the four is told, privately, the impossible thing they have
noticed about Wren and the self-blaming story they invented to survive it — and instructed to say it
out loud, one line each, in seat order. Four confessions, four different gifts, one child. The intended
suspicion is no longer "is something strange about Wren" but **"how many different ways can one person
be impossible, and which of us is right about what it means?"** Five readings should be live
simultaneously, each owned by a different person at the table. This is the high-water mark of the
game's hypothesis space and the beat the whole evening is built on.

**T3 — the Vigil.** Politics, and the first real loss. Nine banners, nine Masters, a royal writ, and a
vote the table can lose. The intended feeling is **outnumbered and out-bought**: two seats are paid, one
is blocked, one is deaf, two are already committed, and the school's default is to give the child away.
The intended suspicions are: *this institution is compromised*; *the Provost knows something about the
fire she is not saying, and she is looking at the child instead of it*; *the Envoy is leveraging her
personally, and he is telling the truth about something.* The table should leave Chapter I with a
mission, a clock, and one unpaid promise from the Envoy: **ask your Seer what is under the paint.**

**T4 — the vault.** Wonder into dread. The table goes under the school and finds, with their own hands,
that the evidence was tampered with: four hooded statues turned off their own holes, four names worn
away, a floor newer than the room, a road bricked shut with a number cut beside it. The intended feeling
is **archaeological**: they are not being told about a cover-up, they are standing in it, and the
Binder is the one who dates it. If they look behind the plinths they find a Founder's name and, on the
shared screen, the correction the whole game turns on — *four, as one, went through. Not one.* The
intended suspicion: **the school's reading of the prophecy is not a scholarly error, it is a cover
story**, and somebody in Year 212 paid for it in stone.

**T5 — the gallery and the laundry.** Twenty minutes of quiet, tense logistics, then the best five
minutes at the table. Wren pulls each of the four aside in the dark and asks one question — and each
player answers alone, on a phone, and nobody will ever know what anybody said. The intended feeling is
**a private moral problem in a public room**. Then: "Thank you. All of you. Even the ones who lied." The
intended suspicion shifts from *what is Wren* to **what do we owe Wren** — and, quietly, *Wren already
knew all four answers and wanted to hear who would say them.*

**T6 — the study.** The richest room in the game, and the one where the player's model of an adult
breaks open. Four corners, four gifts, four classes of evidence: a journal that calls a child *it* in
the same two sentences that are unmistakably tender; a bell that keeps every voice in the room but one;
a picture under the paint of four people walking into a fire with no child in it; and a thread that has
been grey for fourteen years. The intended feeling is **sympathy arriving at exactly the wrong time**:
the table is now certain Marrow will spend Wren, and equally certain she loves Wren, and the game
refuses to let those be two things. Then she asks them to swear. The intended suspicion: **the Founders
paid something called *grey*, one of them was never asked, and the Crown wants the wound open rather
than shut.**

**T7 — the Long Stair.** The premise is finally said out loud by an adult: *under this school there is a
wound; the fire is going out; tonight I take the child down and shut it again.* The intended feeling is
**descent and cost** — the world opens into an inverted sky with four empty thrones over a drowned hall,
and then somebody at the table has to give up their Sight for an hour to hold a door. This is where the
game stops being about Wren's price and starts being about theirs. The intended suspicion, at its
strongest and most productive, is the wrong one: **the fire is dying because of something, and the most
obvious candidate is the child it produced.**

**T8 — the bells.** The chapter that answers the game. Four people stand on an iron lid with the Cold
under it and ring a four-hundred-year-old pattern in the dark, one voice calling and three ringing —
which is to say they *perform the Founders* before they are told what the Founders became. Then the
Provost lays her hands flat and says the sentence the whole night has been built to reach: *It is held.
Not closed — held. The last of it is not mine to do.* Then Wren refuses to walk in order to ask four
friends four questions in public. Then the fire drops far enough for the foot of the prophecy stone to
be lit from below for the first time in four hundred years, and the table reads it themselves: **Four,
as one, go down with fire. What is kept stays behind. The fire is the hollow they left.** The intended
feeling is catharsis and then cold. The intended understanding, at last correct: **the fire is four
people, it is dying because four people is a finite quantity of fire, and nobody did anything wrong.**

**T9 — the Finale.** Decision, not deduction. The table argues, with no clock, about who pays. The
intended feeling is **the weight of an informed, irreversible choice made by four people who like each
other** — and, at its peak, a fourteen-year-old stepping into the one socket in an eight-socket ring and
saying, of the cold: *It's me.* The name in that socket is four hundred years older than the alphabet,
and one player has been holding that fact on a phone since one beat earlier. The intended suspicion has
become a resolution the table reached before the game said it — the best clause in the game is the
Hearth's own acknowledgement: **"Nothing here looks surprised."** Then four hands on four keys, and a
struck Law is not quoted but *enacted*.

**T10 — the Epilogue.** The phones give back what they took. The route of the night, drawn honestly for
two hours, turns out to be the glyph KNOT — *bound; together; four-as-one*. The seven chapter words
turn out to be every glyph that can be written, and the eighth is Wren. Four sealed boxes reveal who
lied to a child in a laundry and who took a mastership in the dark, or the table chooses to leave them
sealed. Then each phone shows one last private page, and burns: **"Your Sighting is spent. Look up."**
→ *"There is nothing left on this page. Look at the people at the table."* The intended feeling is
that the game has been about the four people in the room the whole time, and has just said so by
deleting itself.

## 4.1 What the central reveal needs — **III-P / II-H**, restated for the author

Aha **P** is the game's title, its thesis, and its least-earned reveal. It is delivered as a single
narration clause at `ch6.js:839` to a player who has been given nothing to prepare for it, and two
shipped pieces of evidence point the other way. Every repair below uses material that already exists.

**Five upstream (Act II):**

1. **A name on a throne-back at `ch5_marches`.** *Mere.* Four thrones, four Founders — and the player
   has already met a name "cut fresh on the back" of a plinth in ch2, so the idiom is established. It
   puts four individuals under the school and answers §12.25 and §12.38 at once.
2. **The four names carved over the Hearth.** E2 asserts they exist ("a fifth name over the Hearth,
   **beneath the four Founders**") and no chapter ever shows them. **Show them once, in Act II.** *A fire
   with four names over it* is one inference from the answer and is not the answer.
3. **Mere's "after".** Nobody in the fiction remarks that a woman who walked into the Cold signed a sheet
   afterwards. Wren, on reading it: *"'After.' She came back out."*
4. **The Hearth's blue core.** By ch6 the fire is drawn with a pale-blue heart and ch5's Hearth is
   already "a blue tongue the height of a hand". Let the Seer say it: *"There is cold inside the fire.
   There has been all night."*
5. **The Listener's portraits, as a chorus.** Let the mutter change across the night — ch3 "four went
   down", ch5 "four went down and four came back", ch6 "four went down and four stayed". One word per
   chapter, and the line that currently argues *against* P starts arguing *for* it.

**Two in-span (Act III), both one clause, both using material already on screen:**

6. **`ch6_start`, the Seer:** *"There is cold inside that fire, and there has been all night."* The only
   public evidence for P available in Act III.
7. **`ch6_tieoff`, Marrow, five words:** *"One of them heard, the way you do."* Once the Founders are
   four *people with four gifts* rather than four *founders*, "the fire is only what they left behind"
   becomes a conclusion rather than a sentence.

## 4.2 The acknowledgement problem — the one systemic defect in the ledger

Three ahas (**II-B / III-O**, **II-F**, **III-U**) are *over-earned*: a table that talks can reach them
one to four chapters early. In exactly **one** place does the game notice — `ch7.js:698`, "**Nothing here
looks surprised.**" Everywhere else, the best-prepared table receives **less** than an ignorant one: the
same reveal, delivered as news. Three conditional lines fix all three:

| aha | the line | condition |
|---|---|---|
| II-B / III-O | Marrow: *"Some of you have known this since the vault."* | `CH2_STRIP='right'` or `TAPESTRY` |
| II-F | Wren: *"You all knew. You have all known for a while. Good. That saves a speech."* | `CLUES === 4` |
| III-U | *"The Binder said this ten minutes ago and nobody argued."* | Sigil closed first time |

## 6.2 The register's shape, and what it says about the game

**Twenty-six entries, and they cluster.** Eleven of the top fifteen are one of three kinds:

1. **An object or a promise the game raises loudly and then stops tracking** — the Ember (W-1), the
   Masters' Sightings (W-20), Oriel's price (W-23), the niche (W-8), Vane after the wall (W-6),
   Marrow at the ending (W-2). *The game is excellent at introducing and poor at retiring.*
2. **A word used in two senses and never disambiguated** — *grey* (W-7), *spent* (W-7/H-SPENDISREVERSIBLE),
   *Vigil ward* (W-24), *WREN/WRENN* (§13.5). *Each costs one clause and each is currently a player-visible
   inconsistency rather than a mystery.*
3. **A mechanism the fiction declines to have anybody ask about** — how Wren knows (I-A8), who built the
   lid (W-11), who chalked the name (W-26), how Marrow knows the lock (W-13), why the ring is "ready"
   (W-18), what a Sealing is (W-22). *In every one of these the repair is not an answer but a
   **question**: one character noticing out loud that nobody asked.* That is the single most repeatable
   fix in this document, and `§12.21`'s proposed line — *"Nobody asks how Wren knows… Nobody ever has"* —
   is its template.

**The cost of the whole register is roughly thirty sentences.**

## 7.3 The pattern worth recording

**Every puzzle in the game that teaches well teaches the *same* thing — that four is a requirement and
not a theme — and teaches it in a different modality.**

| modality | where | what four means there |
|---|---|---|
| **motor skill** | `ch0_keys` | four hands, one second, or nothing happens |
| **grammar** | `ch0_lamp` | four kinds of knowledge, none of them optional |
| **law** | `ch2_door` | four Laws, and the older one binds |
| **picture** | `ch4_shelf`, `ch4_secrets` | four figures; four corners; one turned board |
| **rhythm** | `ch6_round1` | four lanes, inside a breath |
| **rhythm, blind** | `ch6_round3` | one voice, three hands, no light |
| **geometry** | `ch6_strip` | eight cuts round a ring with no first cut, read by four gifts |
| **one simultaneous press** | `ch7_fourhands` | the struck Law, enacted |

**That escalation is the best structural decision in the game and no document currently records it.**
It belongs in `DESIGN.md`. It is also the reason the ending works on people who could not reconstruct a
single Law: by hour four the table has learned *with its hands*, eight different ways, that this world
does not let one person finish anything.
