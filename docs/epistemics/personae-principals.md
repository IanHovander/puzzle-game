# PERSONAE — WREN, MARROW, VANE, and the four

> ## ⚠ TOTAL SPOILERS
> Every reveal in *What the Fire Keeps*, including the Prologue's, the Finale's and all five endings.
> Written for the author. Do not show a player or a host. Companion to `CANON.md` (ground truth);
> destined for `EPISTEMICS.md` §persona.

**Scope.** Wren; Provost Ilsabet Marrow; Lord Cassian Vane; and the four player roles (Reader,
Listener, Seer, Binder). Supporting cast appears only as the *object* of a principal's belief or
feeling — their own matrices are not in this file.

**The four layers, and the rule that keeps them apart.** A layer may only be sourced from the kind of
evidence in its right-hand column. A claim sourced from the wrong column is a leak, and a leak is how
these documents rot.

| layer | the question | may be sourced from | may **never** be sourced from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 · ACTUAL** | what they are really like | narration; art; mechanics; what they *do* under branch; a Sighting's factual block | their own claim about themselves |
| **2 · SELF-IMAGE** | what they believe they are like | their own speech and writing; what they do unprompted and unwatched | narration's verdict on them |
| **3 · BELIEVED REPUTATION** | what they think *each named other* thinks of them | how they address that person; what they pre-empt, apologise for, or refuse to say to them | what that person actually thinks |
| **4 · FEELING** | what they feel / what they show / the gap | both halves cited **separately**, never one cite for both | inference from layer 2 |

**Marks.** `[PROPOSED]` = my inference; author to rule. `[GAP]` = the game does not supply it and the
absence costs something. `[WRONG]` = a shipped line that contradicts the character the rest of the
game builds. `[CONTRADICTION]` / `[UNEARNED]` / `[WITHHOLDING]` as in `CANON.md`. Branch flags in
`SMALL CAPS` as in `CANON.md` Appendix A.

**Citations** are `file:line` into the shipped source, `js/content/` implied. First use in a section is
long-form; after that, short.

**The pronoun.** The brief says "his". Every Hearth line in the shipped game is scrupulously
pronoun-free about Wren; only Vane and his soldiers say "the boy"/"he" (`ch1.js:280-282`;
`ch3.js:204`, `:543`; `ch7.js:315-316`). Two companion lines break it in opposite directions
(`companion/ch5.js:290` "her"; `companion/ch8.js:195` "him") — `CANON.md` §13.4. **This document keeps
the house convention** (Wren / Wren's) in analysis, and uses "his" only where the brief's phrasing is
being answered directly. The convention is almost certainly diegetic (§12.27a): the narration declines
a pronoun because Wren is not, until the last twenty minutes, a person of whom one is true. See §1.8g.

### 1.1b The defining contradiction

> **Wren runs the entire night by instruction and has never once made a request — and the thing Wren
> wants most is to be asked for.**

The evidence for the first half is W1: forty-odd imperatives and not one "please" or "will you" on any
branch. The evidence for the second half exists in exactly two places, both of them **after it is too
late to act on**:

- ENDING 2 — "It is alright. **I knew. I wanted to hear what you would say.**" `ch7.js:768`
- ENDING 0 — "You *idiots*. **I had a *speech*.**" `ch7.js:759`, with "Wren is on the warm stones,
  crying, and will deny it." `:758`

The speech Wren had prepared was the one that was never given, because the four moved first. Wren
spent nine chapters arranging a night in which no one would ever have to *choose* to save him — and
the only ending in which Wren is happy is the one where they chose anyway, unprompted, and Wren is
furious about it.

**Why it is a contradiction and not a theme.** A person who wants to be chosen and does not ask is
ordinary. Wren goes further: Wren actively *removes the opportunity to choose* — absolving the
bargain-taker before the bargain is scored (`ch7.js:499`), lying about the warm room (`ch1.js:302`),
telling the four not to make faces (`ch7.js:405`), and giving Marrow permission to confess so that she
is not the one carrying it (`ch6.js:689`). Wren is generous in a way that is, structurally, a refusal
to be loved on terms Wren did not write.

### 1.1d What the game never permits Wren to be

Worth stating, because it is a design constraint and not a character fact:

- **Cruel.** Not once, on any branch — except by silence, once, on E4 (`ch8.js:354`), where the game
  names it as cruelty.
- **Wrong.** Every Wren diagnosis in the game is correct. There is no line where Wren misreads a
  person or a room. **[PROPOSED]** this is a cost: it makes Wren an instrument of the narration.
- **Angry at an adult.** Wren is brittle with Vane (`ch7.js:433`) and never angry with Marrow.
- **Pronouned.** §1.8g.

| feature | the rule as shipped |

### 1.5b The subjects Wren circles

1. **The four's gifts.** Named, praised and used, in every chapter, as the currency of affection.
2. **Procedure.** "Has everyone actually said their bit?" — six times, in six chapters.
3. **His own hollowness, at one remove and always as a noun:** the occasion, the hollow, the thing they're coming to look at, the bit that isn't written, a gap in the middle (`ch5.js:403`).
4. **Marrow's habits**, in the third person, to other people (`ch4.js:344`, `:497`, `:524`).
5. **The fire**, as a person (`ch6.js:473`).
6. **Being forgotten or misremembered** — "I want to remember it" (`ch4.js:770`); "Tell the Provost I said the corridors were *easy*" (`ch3.js:633`); "Write to me" (`ch7.js:782`).

### 1.5c The one subject Wren will not touch

**Wanting to be kept.** Not fear — fear is shown mechanically (`WREN_SCARED`). Not death — Wren
discusses the walk as a schedule. **Wanting.** Wren does not want anything out loud, ever, on any
branch, until it cannot be acted on. The two exceptions (`ch7.js:759`, `:768`) both arrive *after the
decision is sealed*, and one of them is disguised as a complaint.

The second-order untouchable: **other people's costs.** Wren interrupts, absolves or jokes over every
attempt by anyone to weigh what they are giving up. (`ch7.js:499`; `ch1.js:302`; `ch6.js:689`.)

### 1.5d Three lines that are perfectly Wren

1. **"You're awake. Good. I need four idiots and a lamp."** — `ch0.js:145`
   *Imperative, affectionate insult, a plan, and a request smuggled in as a requirement. Twelve words
   containing the whole character.*
2. **"That was kind. It was not true, and I would rather have had the true one."** — `ch6.js:320`
   *The only moral position Wren holds, said to someone who has just tried to be gentle with him, with
   no italic and no joke. This is what Wren sounds like with the armour off, which is why it is
   fifteen words long and appears once.*
3. **"It's cold in here. Obviously. It's me."** — `ch7.js:697`
   *The reveal of the game delivered as a joke about the temperature, in three sentences of
   descending length. The "Obviously" is the whole comic engine in four syllables.*

**Runner-up, and the best single-clause Wren in the game:** "I'd have taken it too." `ch7.js:499` —
said to a friend who has just tried to sell him.

### 1.5e One line currently in the game that is **not** Wren

> `js/content/ch7.js:764` — ENDING 1 — **Wren: "Half a walk. Story of my life."**

**[WRONG].** Three separate failures:

1. **It is a stock idiom.** Every other joke Wren makes is specific to the room he is standing in —
   the lamp, the study's fire, the thrones, the size of the Hearth from below, the phone he does not
   have. "Story of my life" could be said by anybody in any fiction. Wren's comedy is *observational
   and local*; this is a greeting card.
2. **It is self-pitying, and Wren's jokes are never self-pitying.** The rule (W4/W6) is that a Wren
   joke costs Wren and spares the room. This one asks the room for sympathy. Compare the same beat on
   E0 — "You *idiots*. I had a *speech*." — which is an *accusation of generosity*, and is correct.
3. **It is placed on the worst possible beat.** Two or three of the four have just permanently spent
   their Sight for him (`ch8.js:319-321`), and the first thing out of Wren's mouth is a complaint
   about Wren's luck.

**Proposed replacement** (same length, same beat, same italic budget):

> **Wren: "You didn't all have to. *Half* of you didn't have to."**

Or, if the author wants the joke kept: **"Half of you. I'll take half."** — which is a joke, is about
the room, and is a thank-you disguised as an appraisal.

**Runners-up for rewrite, in order:** `ch5.js:604` "For the record, I said we should collapse it." is
fine but is immediately undercut by `ch5.js:605` "Nobody remembers Wren saying that" — a narration
line with no receipt anywhere (`CANON.md` §12.63); and `ch8.js:346` "Wren, **who once called someone
Mum by accident**" is flatly false (§13.13) and is narration *about* Wren rather than Wren's voice.

## 1.7 WREN'S WORST MOMENT

### 1.7a Where the reader should like Wren least

> **The Prologue, `ch0.js:226-227`:** *"Yes. All four of you. I've known for years."* / *"It's fine.
> You can stop pretending you didn't notice."*

Read against the four private pages that have just been shown to four players:

- the Listener "decided years ago that **the fault was yours**, and you have never said it out loud to
  anyone" — `companion/ch0.js:112`
- the Seer "decided months ago it was a trick of the light" — `:124`
- the Binder "decided it was a blind spot in your own gift. **You have never told anyone your gift has
  a blind spot.**" — `:145`
- the Reader "decided, a year ago, that somebody was being funny. **You have never asked who.**" — `:99`

Wren has watched four friends each quietly conclude that something is wrong *with them*, for years,
and has said nothing — and then announces it as a punch line, with "It's fine", and moves the
conversation on to choosing a group name (`ch0.js:228`). It is the one moment in the game where Wren's
management of other people's feelings is indistinguishable from using them.

### 1.7b Does the game currently have one?

**Yes, but in the wrong place and on the wrong axis.**

| candidate | where | does the game mark it? |
|---|---|---|
| The Prologue announcement (§1.7a) | `ch0.js:226-227` — **every branch** | **No.** Narration says nothing. Nobody in the fiction asks how Wren knew (`CANON.md` §12.21), and nobody says "you could have told me" |
| Knowing things only a single seat can perceive, and using them to recruit | `ch0.js:172-174` | **No** — and this is the same hole viewed from the mechanics side |
| The E4 silence | `ch8.js:354` | **Yes, explicitly** — "It is the worst thing Wren has ever done." But it is **branch-gated to the worst ending**, where the reader is already being punished, and it is framed as Wren's *withdrawal of forgiveness*, which most readers will read as justified |
| Absolving the bargain-taker | `ch7.js:499` | **No.** It reads as grace. It is also a pre-emptive refusal to let a friend own a betrayal |

### 1.7c The design problem, stated plainly

A character the reader is never allowed to dislike is not trusted by the author. Wren is currently
protected on every universal path: every unkind thing Wren does is either invisible (§1.7a),
unremarked (`ch0.js:172-174`), or re-labelled as generosity (`ch7.js:499`). The only sanctioned
dislike is quarantined inside the ending the player has already been told is the bad one.

### 1.7d Two proposed fixes, both one clause **[PROPOSED]**

1. **Mark the Prologue.** One narration line after `ch0.js:227`, in the game's own voice, of the kind
   it already uses for Wren (`ch1.js:303`; `ch2.js:376`; `ch8.js:262`):

   > *"Four people have each been quietly wrong about themselves for years, and Wren has known, and
   > has let them. Nobody says so. Nobody will."*

   This converts the game's largest unearned moment (§12.21) into its first plant, costs nothing, and
   gives the reader permission to be annoyed with Wren before the game asks them to love him.

2. **Give Wren one reproach.** Anywhere in ch6 after `ch6.js:692`, a single clause to Marrow — not a
   speech, not an accusation, and immediately withdrawn:

   > Wren: **"Loved enough. Right. — No, say the rest of it."**

   The gap in §1.4 (Wren→Marrow) is currently unclosed on all five endings. One clause closes it and
   costs Marrow nothing she is not already paying.

## 1.8 THE COMIC ENGINE

The author asked for the mechanism. Here it is, in six parts.

### 1.8a The trigger

Wren makes a joke when **somebody in the room is about to feel something about Wren**. Not when Wren
feels something — when *they* do. The joke is not a response to danger; it is a response to attention.

Check it against every instance:

| beat | who was about to feel something | the joke |
|---|---|---|
| the dais, nine Houses staring | the whole hall, and the four at the back | "I'll try not to fidget." `ch1.js:127` |
| soldiers on the step, VOTE_LOST | the four, who have just lost the vote | "They have a warm room. I've never had a warm room." `ch1.js:302` |
| a broken arm, hauled up on a cloak | the four, who chose the box | "I'm fine. It's only my arm. **Don't look like that.**" `ch2.js:375` |
| handed to the captain | the four, who surrendered him | "Tell the Provost I said the corridors were *easy*." `ch3.js:633` |
| the journal that calls him *it* | the four, reading it | "She writes *it*. And then she writes that." `ch4.js:497` |
| the grey thread | the Binder, who has just named a colour | "Grey is a colour. I have seen grey. Grey is fine." `ch4.js:594` |
| four empty thrones | everyone | "Four thrones. Four Founders. It is a *theme*." `ch5.js:349` |
| the Founders' Count, which Wren cannot play | the four, mid-triumph | "I would have got two of them. I do not have a phone." `ch5.js:479` |

| two friends left grey-eyed for him | the four | "I looked *terrible*." `ch8.js:323` |

Twelve for twelve. **The joke arrives in the half-second before someone would otherwise have to say
something kind.**

### 1.8b What it is defending against

Not death, not the Cold, not the Convocation. **Being the object of somebody's pity — because pity is
the form attention takes when the attention is about what Wren *is*, and what Wren is, is a hollow.**

The mechanism is visible in the syntax: the joke almost always contains an instruction not to feel
("Don't look like that", "Don't do faces", "Do not tell it I said that", "for the record"). The joke
is a *stage direction to the audience*, issued by the person on stage.

It is also, secondarily, defending the four from the knowledge that they are being managed — §1.1b.

### 1.8c How it changes under pressure — the four gears

| gear | pressure | what the comedy does | cite |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 · ambient** | none | Diagnosis-as-endearment; observational; punchline last | `ch0.js:146-149`; `ch5.js:349` |
| **2 · loaded** | somebody is about to be kind | The instruction appears inside the joke — "Don't look like that", "Don't do faces" | `ch2.js:375`; `ch7.js:405` |
| **3 · overloaded** | something is actually happening to Wren's body or future | **Stacking.** "Also… Also… Also —" and the sentence is abandoned rather than finished | `ch2.js:360` |
| **4 · off** | the thing has happened | **Silence, and the game names it.** Or the mechanical kill-switch: `WREN_SCARED` removes every Wren joke from Chapter IV — the game's only comedy flag | `ch8.js:262`, `:354`; `ch3.js:234` → `ch4.js:79`, `:342` |

**The key inversion:** most comic characters get funnier under pressure. Wren gets *shorter*, then
*stops*. The reader learns, by ch4, that a quiet Wren is an emergency. This is why `WREN_SCARED` is
worth more than any line it deletes: it teaches the grammar of Wren's silence one chapter before the
game needs the reader to read it.

### 1.8d What the jokes are actually *about*

Sorted by frequency. This is the tell: Wren's jokes have almost no subjects, and they repeat.

| subject | share | examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Wren's own non-personhood** | the largest class | "the occasion" `ch5.js:254` · "four idiots and a hollow" `ch7.js:423` · "the bit that isn't written" `ch7.js:405` · "a gap in the middle. I would have written something." `ch5.js:403` · "It's cold in here. Obviously. It's me." `ch7.js:697` |
| **Wren's exclusion from the four's apparatus** | second | "I do not have a phone." `ch5.js:479` · "We had a lamp." `ch4.js:344` · "I would have got two of them." `ch5.js:479` |
| **adults being absurd on their own terms** | third | "Cheerful woman, Mere." `ch5.js:337` · "She has a *fire*. In her study." `ch4.js:344` · "*Sorrel* saved me? I called her a goat once." `ch3.js:405` |
| **the four being formidable** | fourth, and it is affection | "Like a very small general." `ch3.js:491` · "That was the best thing I have ever seen." `ch3.js:607` · "The Binder doesn't walk up to anyone." `ch1.js:232` |
| **the fire** | once | `ch6.js:473` |

**Note what is missing:** Wren never jokes about the Cold, about dying, about the Crown, or about
Marrow's love. The comedy has a strict exclusion zone and it maps exactly onto §1.5c.

### 1.8e The swerve

**Every Wren joke about being a hollow swerves at the last moment away from the fact that being a
hollow is a thing that happened *to a baby*.** Wren will call himself the occasion, the hollow, the
gap, the bit that isn't written — all of them nouns, all of them present tense, all of them
descriptions of a *shape*. Wren never once refers to **the night the fire went out**, the only event
in the world that is about Wren, and the one every other character talks about freely (`ch0.js:51-53`;
`ch1.js:123`; `ch6.js:691`; `ch8.js:330`).

Wren jokes about *what I am*. Wren does not go near *where I came from*. The whole comic apparatus is
built at the distance of one noun from that sentence.

### 1.8f The economy — how many jokes, and where they stop

Roughly: ch0 heavy (7), ch1 two, ch2 three, ch3 five, ch4 four or **zero** (`WREN_SCARED`), ch5 six,
ch6 one, ch7 three, ch8 one per ending. **The comedy thins toward the centre of the night and stops
almost entirely in ch6**, the chapter where Wren asks four questions and gets four answers. That is
correct and deliberate, and it means the ch6 crack in §1.9 has to be even smaller than the others.

### 1.8g One consequence worth naming

The house convention of never pronouning Wren (§0) is the *narration's* version of the same joke.
The narration, too, is declining to say what Wren is, using a formal device, for nine chapters. When
Wren gets a pulse (`ch8.js:290`) the game still does not use a pronoun — which means the one thing the
game never grants is the thing the comic engine is built to avoid needing. **[PROPOSED]** If the
author ever wants a single devastating beat, it is one pronoun, once, in the last line of E0.

## 1.9 THE NINE CRACKS — where the levity should break for exactly one clause

**The rule.** A crack is **one clause, never a sentence**. It arrives **inside** a joke, not after it.
It is about **wanting**, not about fear. And **nothing in the scene stops to acknowledge it** — the
next clause re-arms, or the next speaker simply carries on. If a crack needs a reaction line, it is
too big.

**The shipped precedent** (this technique is already in the game, on the phones, and works):

- `companion/ch3.js:289` — "*'Right. Okay. Thank you for not — right.'*" **The kettle covers whatever
  comes next.**
- `companion/ch3.js:295` — "*'Nobody's ever said that to me. Everyone always knows.'* And then, almost
  too low to hear: *'Thanks.'*"

Both are Wren, both are one clause, both are immediately buried. The nine below put the same device on
the Hearth screen, where the whole table hears it.

### 2.1b The defining contradiction

> **She is the only adult in the game who will change her mind for a reason, and the only adult who
> never offered the child an alternative she could have argued for herself.**

Both halves are load-bearing and both are hers:

- She yields to *every* argument at `ch7.js:391-398`, including "Because it is Wren, and we are not
  doing it", which she names as not-a-reading and accepts anyway.
- She has been able to read the prophecy stone from its foot the whole time (`ch6.js:824`). Fourteen
  years of knowing that the stone says *four, as one* (`ch6.js:827-828`), and fourteen years of
  raising one child to walk in alone.

She did not lie to Wren. She simply never opened the conversation, and then built a person who would
not ask her to. **The thread went grey the night she picked the child up** (`ch8.js:333`) — that is,
she decided the ending before the child could talk, and has spent fourteen years being right about her
own grief.

**A second, smaller contradiction worth keeping:** she is the only person in the game who kneels, and
the only person in the game nobody in the fiction dares interrupt.

### 2.5b The subjects she circles

1. **Cost and time.** "Midnight is ninety minutes off"; "Twelve turns is all I can buy you"; "That is two minutes you will want at the bottom."
2. **Mere and the Founders' practice.** Blind ringing; one caller and three ringers; how the stone was cut; how Mere's gates behave.
3. **What she will do herself.** "I will get the child back myself"; "I have put out every lamp… myself"; "I will do the rest"; "Then I go."
4. **The four's obligations.** Oath, hands, keys, hold, walk.

### 2.5c The one subject she will not touch

**The fourteen years.** She names the number six times and never once says what was in them. Every use
is a measurement of somebody else's obligation or her own failure — "It has not done that in fourteen
years" (`ch1.js:309`); "The ring has been ready for fourteen years" (`ch7.js:367`); "I should have gone
fourteen years ago" (`ch7.js:777`); "it cost fourteen years" (`companion/ch8.js:196`). The one thing
that happened in them — a child sleeping with the window open and laughing at her jokes — exists only
in a private journal in a dead alphabet, which she left open on her desk under her own primer
(`ch4.js:51`; `:211`).

### 2.5d Three lines that are perfectly Marrow

1. **"This school does not hand its children to a writ. It hands them to a vote. Nine seats. Five
   keeps."** — `ch1.js:146`
   *Four sentences, descending from twelve words to two. A constitutional position, a procedure and a
   scoreline, with no adjective and no defiance anywhere in it.*
2. **"Miss it and the Cold pushes further. That is all that happens. Hands on your keys."** —
   `ch6.js:488`
   *The most honest sentence any adult says in the game. She removes the terror of failure by pricing
   it exactly, and then gives an order. Note that she is de-escalating for children she is about to
   ask to hold a wound shut.*
3. **"Now, love. Walk."** — `ch6.js:630`
   *Three words. The only endearment she permits herself in nine chapters, and it is the hinge of an
   instruction to go and be spent. The comma is the entire character.*

**Runner-up:** "Mere. Forgive me. There is a child on this stair." `ch5.js:395` — an apology to a
four-hundred-year-dead colleague, with the justification attached, while destroying her work.

### 2.5e One line currently in the game that is **not** Marrow

> `js/content/ch6.js:823` — **Provost Marrow: "Four readings. Stop. Hands off it — I have had four
> hundred years of this stone and you have had five minutes."**

**[WRONG].** Four failures:

1. **It is a boast**, and Marrow never makes a claim about her own standing. Every first-person
   sentence she has elsewhere is an order, a cost or an indictment (§2.1a M20).
2. **It is 24 words** — double her register, and the only line in the game where she uses a rhetorical
   flourish to win a point.
3. **It is literally false and unusably so.** She has not had four hundred years of anything. As
   hyperbole it is fine in most mouths; in hers it is the one time she says something inexact, in a
   chapter whose subject is a misreading that has stood for four hundred years because somebody was
   inexact on purpose.
4. **It is unkind at the wrong moment.** The four have just failed the game's most important puzzle
   and she is about to do for them the thing she could have done at any point in fourteen years. She
   takes the stone off them with a put-down, when the scene needs her to take it off them with a cost.

**Proposed replacement** (same beat, same function, her register, and it makes `ch6.js:824` land):

> **Provost Marrow: "Four readings. Stop. Hands off it."**
> *She kneels at the foot, puts one thumb in the first burn, and reads it the way the Founders cut
> it.*

If the author wants the cost stated rather than implied, one clause, not a boast:

> **"Four readings. Stop. Hands off it — I should have read it to you in the study."**

which is her actual register (M15: self-indicting, afterwards, in the imperative) and converts the
losing branch from a humiliation into her confession.

**Runner-up for rewrite:** `ch4.js:355` "After tonight I will decide what you are." (`SURRENDERED`) is
in-register but is the one Marrow line with no follow-through anywhere — she never decides, and
nothing in ch5–ch8 refers to it. Either pay it or cut it. **[GAP]**

## 2.7 MARROW'S WORST MOMENT

### 2.7a Where the reader should like her least

> **`ch6.js:691-692`** — *"It came out of the fire the night the Hearth guttered. I picked it up. I
> named it. I raised it to be—"* / *"'Loved,' says Wren. '**Loved enough to walk back in**,' says

Four sentences of increasing tenderness, each beginning with "I", each a verb done *to* an object

the true one. It is the moment the reader learns that the love was never in tension with the plan

### 2.7c Does the game currently have one? — **Yes, and then it takes it back**

The game gives Marrow a genuinely dislikeable centre (§2.7a) and then, **158 lines later, in her own
mouth**, absolves her:

> `ch6.js:850` — "Four people's worth of fire, and four hundred years to spend it in. That is the

The line is correct about the Hearth. But it is the **thesis of the chapter** and it is spoken by the

plainly intended to read — as absolution of everyone in the room including herself. The reader's
permission to dislike her lasts a page and a half.

### 2.7d Two proposed fixes **[PROPOSED]**

1. **Move the line, or move the mouth.** `ch6.js:850` is the game's answer to "why is the fire going

   already speaks in its own voice at `ch6.js:839`, the reveal itself), and leave Marrow with the
   clause the chapter earned her: *"It was only ever four people."* Six words, in register, and it no
   longer sounds like a woman telling four children that nobody is to blame while kneeling in front of

2. **Let one of the four dislike her out loud, once.** There is currently **no line anywhere in the
   game in which any character criticises Marrow to her face** except Wren's affectionate mockery

### 3.1b The defining contradiction

> **He is the only adult in the game who ever told the Convocation the truth, and the only adult in
> the game buying its seats — and he cannot see that the second is why they were right to distrust the
> first.**

for it (`ch7.js:341`). He came back with a writ, two bought Masters (5 and 8, `companion/ch1.js:125-126`),
a soldier standing behind a third (`:127`), a bought porter (`companion/ch3.js:239`), and four private

everything he has done since**, and the game never lets him notice the connection.

**The second contradiction, which is his best feature:** he will not lie, and he legislates for being
lied to. "Wise. Or a lie. **I can use either.**" (`ch1.js:291`) is a man who has made honesty
structurally irrelevant and has not noticed that this is the thing he was exiled for objecting to.

### 3.5a Mechanics

| feature | the rule as shipped |
|---|---|
| **register** | Formal, unhurried, complete sentences with the subject first. No contractions anywhere in his dialogue. No exclamation, ever |

1. **"His Majesty asks one small thing: the child, tonight, for safekeeping."** — `ch1.js:136`

2. **"Wise. Or a lie. I can use either."** — `ch1.js:291`

   characterisation in the game.*
3. **"My offer is withdrawn. I will not be the thing you have to be brave about."** — `ch7.js:342`

**Runner-up:** "Nothing. Well. Nothing is an answer." `ch7.js:491` — a man scoring a fifteen-second
silence.

### 3.5e One line currently in the game that is **not** Vane

> `js/content/ch1.js:283` — **Vane: "You think I am the villain of tonight. Ask your Seer what is under
> the paint."**

**[WRONG] in the first sentence only.** The second half is perfect Vane (V9) and must stay.

1. **It names the genre.** A man of Vane's manners does not say "villain". It is the only
   self-conscious, theatrical word in his mouth in nine chapters, and it tells the player how to feel
   about a character whose entire power is that the player cannot decide.
2. **It states a believed reputation instead of manoeuvring around it** — the one thing he never does
   anywhere else (§3.3). Vane's method is to *point at evidence and decline to explain it*; he has
   never once defended himself.
3. **It pre-empts the player's own judgement at the exact moment the game wants that judgement to be
   uncomfortable.** The offer scene has a sixty-second timer (`ch1.js:273-276`) and four children are
   about to decide whether to sell a friend. Telling them "you think I am the villain" resolves the
   ambiguity for them.
4. **It makes his best line worse.** `ch7.js:342` lands because he has never previously discussed his
   own standing. He has, once, in ch1.

**Proposed replacement** (same length, same function — it still routes the four to the Seer, still
plants the tapestry, and keeps the "he is right" reveal intact):

> **Vane: "You have decided about me already. Ask your Seer what is under the paint, and decide
> again."**

or, shorter and colder, which is more him:

> **Vane: "Before you answer — ask your Seer what is under the paint."**

**Runner-up for rewrite:** `ch7.js:494` "I keep my promises. Do you keep yours?" is exactly him but is
addressed *by the fire* to a named fourteen-year-old ("The fire says one name out loud", `ch7.js:493`)
after he has promised secrecy in writing (`companion/ch7.js:182`). The *mechanism* betrays him, not the
line — worth one clause in which he notices he has been made to break his own confidentiality. **[GAP]**

## 3.7 VANE'S WORST MOMENT

### 3.7a Where the reader should like him least

> **`ch7.js:493-494`** — *"The fire says one name out loud: {nick}."* / **Vane: "I keep my promises.
> Do you keep yours?"**

A grown man, in front of everyone, holds a named fourteen-year-old to a promise made privately, under
duress, in writing, with an explicit assurance of secrecy — and does it in the register of a man
asking after somebody's honour. He does not threaten. He *audits*. It is the moment where his code —
the thing that makes him likeable — is revealed as an instrument for extracting compliance from
children, and it is worse than anything his captain does.

### 3.7b Runners-up

| moment | cite | why |
|---|---|---|
| **The private letters** | `companion/ch7.js:182` | Four individually addressed offers, by first name, promising secrecy — i.e. he sets four friends against each other and tells each of them they will never be found out |
| **"Then I will ask again later, when it costs more."** | `ch1.js:289` | A threat with no verb in it, delivered to children |
| **"He lives. I promise you that."** | `ch1.js:282` | The clause implies the alternative without ever stating it, which is the most frightening sentence in Chapter I |
| **E4's courtesy** | `ch8.js:353-356` | He keeps every promise exactly, and every promise was a trap. "You are Masters, as promised. **Masters of ash.**" |

### 3.7c Does the game currently have one? — **yes on one path, and none at all on the other**

- On `!VANE_ALLY`, §3.7a fires and the reader has a real reason to dislike him.
- On **`VANE_ALLY`** — set exclusively by `ch7_wall`'s enter (`ch7.js:332`) — he withdraws at
  `ch7.js:342`, the bargain question is deleted, the private letter is removed from every SPEAK page
  (`companion/ch7.js:182`, gated on `!ally`), one figure comes out of the art, and **he never appears
  again on any of the five endings** (`CANON.md` §12.30, §12.32).

That is: **the moment Vane becomes likeable, the game removes him.** The author gets a redemption and
never has to price it. His soldiers are still on the stair (`companion/ch5.js:342`); nothing says what
he does with them, what he tells the Crown, or what happens to a man who withdraws a royal writ on his
own authority.

### 3.7d The bigger hole: he never speaks to Wren

In nine chapters, five endings and every branch, **Lord Vane never addresses Wren once.** He speaks
*about* Wren fourteen times. Wren speaks about him once, and it is frightened (`ch7.js:433`). On E4 he
puts Wren in a cage and is courteous about it to *the four* (`ch8.js:353`).

This is either the best deliberate characterisation in the game or an oversight, and the game gives no
signal which. **[PROPOSED]** make it deliberate, in one clause of narration at `ch7.js:432`, where he
holds his hand out:

> *He says it to the four of you. He has not addressed the child once tonight, and will not.*

# 4 · THE FOUR

**The constraint, restated.** The four are played by real people at a table. The shipped game gives
them **no name, no pronoun, no family, no appearance and no history** — only an id, a seat index, a
gift, a colour, a question word and a blurb (`lore.js:5-10`), and four identical faceless silhouettes
in every chapter of the art (`js/art/scenes-ch1.js:59`; `scenes-ch3.js:42`; `scenes-ch5.js:30`;
`scenes-ch7.js:88`; `scenes-ch8.js:47`). Canon about them is therefore limited to **the role, its
gift, what their phone shows them, and what they have privately decided about Wren.**

Consequently, layers 1–4 below are written **about the role**, not about a person, and the last two
columns of every table are the design surface: *what the game gives a player to perform*, and *where it
gives them nothing*.
